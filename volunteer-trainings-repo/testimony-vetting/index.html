01

Purpose — Why This Matters

A testimony that reaches the platform becomes a public declaration of what God has done. Vetting exists to protect the integrity of that declaration — not to doubt people, but to guard against a guest overstating what happened in the emotion of the moment.

This is care, not suspicion

Confirm what can be confirmed. Gently flag what can't, honestly. Protect the guest from overstating in the moment. Protect the pastorate from unknowingly amplifying an unclear claim. Hand off with the truth either way.

04

The Vetter's Posture

Before any physical check happens.

1
Listen intently first

Let them tell the full account uninterrupted before asking anything. Note specifics — what changed, since when, in what circumstance.

2
Ask, don't assume

"Can you show me?" and "What could you not do before that you can do now?" beat any leading question.

3
Move at their pace

Explain gently what you're checking and why: "I just want to see it for myself so I can pass this on properly."

4
Stay neutral on the outcome

Your job is not to decide whether God healed someone — it's to record what is observably true right now.

5
Slow down rather than conclude

An unclear finding isn't a "no" — it's a legitimate outcome to hand to the pastor honestly.

05

No-Equipment Checks

Four categories, each with its own gentle checks. Open the one you need.

M — Mobility +
  • Baseline account — ask exactly what they couldn't do before: walk, stand unaided, climb stairs, straighten a limb.
  • Gait observation — watch them walk a short distance and turn. Look for smoothness, symmetry, hesitation, favouring one side.
  • Stand-to-sit — ask them to sit and stand from a chair without hands or support, if that was part of the limitation.
  • Active range of motion — they move the joint/limb themselves through its full range — never move it for them. Compare both sides.
  • Aid dependency — if they arrived with a cane/wheelchair, note whether they can now manage a short distance safely without it.
  • Balance — simple unsupported standing for a few seconds if relevant. Stop if they seem unsteady — safety first.
S — Sight +
  • Baseline account — what exactly changed: blurred to clear, no sight to sight, one eye or both, since when.
  • Finger count — hold up fingers a few steps away and ask them to count. Vary the number each time.
  • Reading — ask them to read ordinary text at normal distance: a phone screen, a sign, your name badge.
  • Individually per eye — cover one eye at a time and repeat, so the stronger eye isn't compensating unnoticed.
  • Pupil response — in daylight or with a phone torch, briefly check each pupil reacts to light — quick and gentle.
  • Peripheral awareness — wiggle fingers at the edge of their vision on each side and ask if they notice movement.
P — Pain +
  • Baseline account — where the pain was, severity 0–10 before and now, and what specifically triggered it before.
  • Provoked movement — ask them to demonstrate the exact movement that used to cause pain: reaching, bending, turning.
  • Watch, don't just listen — note non-verbal signs like wincing, guarding, breath-holding, versus free, easy movement.
  • Gentle palpation (with consent) — light pressure over non-sensitive areas only, watching their face for a reaction.
  • Consistency — does relief hold across repeated movement, not just a single hopeful attempt?

Pain is subjective by nature — note function and observable behaviour, not just the number given.

L — Lumps & Bumps +
  • Baseline account — ask them to point to exactly where the lump was, how long it had been there, prior diagnosis.
  • Visual comparison — with consent, in a private setting, compare to the equivalent spot on the other side of the body.
  • Gentle palpation (with consent) — light fingertip pressure only, checking size/mobility compared to what they describe.
  • No private-area checks — anything involving breast, groin, or other intimate areas is never checked — see Safeguarding.

This category needs the most care around consent and privacy — see the Safeguarding section before proceeding.

07

Safeguarding — Non-Negotiable

  • Consent every time, spoken clearly, before any physical contact.
  • Same-gender vetter wherever touch is involved.
  • Never a vetter alone with a guest — a second team member present at all times.
  • No private-area examination, ever, regardless of what is claimed.
  • A minor is never physically checked — take the account only and route to the safeguarding lead with a parent/guardian.
  • Stop immediately if the person is in distress, confused, or uncomfortable.

You are not a substitute for clinical care

If something looks concerning outside the claimed testimony, that becomes its own medical referral — routed to the Medical Coordinator, separate from the vetting outcome.

08

The Verification Format — Handing Off to the Pastor

Before the guest speaks, the vetter verbally briefs the pastor in a short, consistent format — every time.

1
Verified

"This guest has been medically verified by the vetting team."

2
The Issue

State the condition or limitation plainly, in a sentence — what it was, since when.

3
What's Changed

State what was observed just now — not embellished, exactly what was demonstrated.

Full example

"This guest has been medically verified. The issue was [X]. What we've now observed is [Y]." Partial or unverifiable outcomes use the same format, stated honestly — never rounded up.

09

Recording the Outcome

For every testimony vetted.

1
Account given

In the person's own words, as specific as possible.

2
What was observed

Plainly, without embellishment either way.

3
Consistency

Did the demonstration match the account given.

4
Outcome

Confirmed / Partially confirmed / Unable to verify on-site.

5
Referral raised

Any safeguarding or medical follow-up, noted separately.

"Unable to verify" is not a failure

It's the honest result — pass it to the pastor with the same integrity either way.

11

Documenting a Medical Incident

Every guest interaction is someone's whole day. A clear, written record protects them, protects you, and keeps the whole medics chain working as one team.

  • Continuity of care — anyone who picks up the case afterward (Group Lead, Coordinator, stadium medical team) knows exactly what happened and what's already been done.
  • Accountability — a written, timestamped record shows the right steps were followed and the right people were informed.
  • Safeguarding — protects the guest, especially where next-of-kin, hospital transfer, or a vulnerable person is involved.
  • Learning for next time — patterns across incidents help the HOD improve training, staffing, and stadium liaison for future conferences.

Golden rule

One form per incident — even minor ones. If it needed a medical assessment, it needs a form. A quick check that turns out fine still gets logged; it protects you and the guest either way.

The attending Medic completes the form — the person who assessed the guest, not a passer-by. Fill it in at the time of the incident, while details are fresh, and submit it from your phone immediately, on the spot — don't batch incidents for later.

2
Person Details

3
Presenting Signs

4
Assessment

5
Actions & Escalation

6
Outcome

7
Sign-off

Medic, Group Lead, Coordinator.

13

Incident & Person Details

The who, where, and when — get this down first, exactly as it happened.

1. Incident Details +
  • Date and time — as close to the actual moment as possible.
  • Stand / Block, and row or seat if known — precise location speeds up any stadium escalation.
  • Reported by — your name, as the attending Medic.
  • Group Lead and Coordinator notified — name who you told, not just "yes".
2. Person Details +
  • Name and approx. age, if you can get them without pressuring a distressed guest.
  • Guest / Volunteer / Staff — changes who else needs informing.
  • Known medical conditions — ask gently; this can change your assessment.
  • Next of kin contacted? — mark clearly if this is still outstanding.
14

Presenting Signs

Two checklists, kept separate on purpose.

  • Unresponsive / no chest movement
  • Collapse / loss of consciousness
  • Seizure-like symptoms
  • Difficulty breathing or gasping
  • Chest pain or clutching chest
  • Severe confusion or unresponsiveness
  • Bluish lips or very pale skin

Spiritual manifestation (for context, not diagnosis)

Shaking · Collapse · Agitation · Unresponsiveness
Note context only — this never replaces the medical assessment. Medical checks always come first.

16

Outcome, Sign-off & Before You Go Out

Close the loop — this is what makes the record usable afterward.

  • Resolved on site
  • Returned to seat
  • Transferred to stadium medical
  • Taken to hospital

Sign-off

No names logged, no closed incident. Medic, Group Lead, and Coordinator names are what make the record official.

Before You Go Out on Shift

  • Medical assessment always comes first.
  • One form, every incident, filled in on site.
  • Name who you escalated to — every time.
  • Submit the form on your phone the moment it's handled — don't batch incidents.
  • Save your form link or QR code before your shift starts.
  • Ask your Coordinator if you're ever unsure.
