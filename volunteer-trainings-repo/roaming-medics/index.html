01

Primary Responsibility

To provide proactive medical support at entrances, queues, and surrounding areas — ensuring vulnerable guests receive timely assistance before entering the worship arena.

Why this matters

Many risks — dehydration, fatigue, mobility struggles — surface in the queue, long before a guest ever reaches their seat. Catching it here prevents a bigger incident later.

02

Responsibilities

  • Support Crowd Management at entrances.
  • Assist elderly/physically challenged guests in queues & entrances.
  • Support medically vulnerable attendees.
  • Respond to emergencies in queues at the four main entrances.
  • Operate wheelchairs safely.
  • Transport patients when necessary.
  • Refer serious cases to the First Aid Room.
03

Expectations

  • Report for duty before gates open.
  • Inspect wheelchair and medical pack (Team Lead).
  • Patrol assigned entrance.
  • Monitor queues.
  • Respond rapidly.
  • Resume standby position after entry closes.
  • Return equipment after use (Team Lead).

07

Medical Red Flags

Immediately escalate if you observe:

  • Unresponsive patient
  • Severe confusion
  • No chest movement
  • Severe breathing difficulty / gasping
  • Seizure-like symptoms
  • Chest pain / clutching chest
  • Bluish lips
  • Pale, clammy skin

Spiritual indicators (for context, not diagnosis)

Shaking · Collapse · Agitation · Unresponsiveness
N/B: Medical assessment always comes first.

08

Common Medical Emergencies

Dehydration & Heat Exhaustion +

Common in warm weather, crowded spaces, or during long periods of standing in the queue. Prolonged exposure to heat and inadequate fluid intake can impair body function.

Risk factors: hot weather, overcrowding, physical exertion, poor ventilation.

Impact: may result in collapse, confusion, or medical deterioration requiring removal from the area and possible hospital referral.

Allergic Reactions +

These can vary from mild skin irritation to life-threatening airway obstruction (anaphylaxis). Common triggers — food, insect bites, or medication — can cause rapid onset of symptoms.

Risk factors: known allergies, accidental exposure to allergens in food or environment.

Impact: hives or itchy skin, swelling of the face, eyes, tongue, lips. May lead to airway compromise, shock, and in severe cases cardiac arrest if not addressed promptly.

Injuries From Slips, Falls & Trips +

Frequent in high-traffic queue areas, especially on wet or uneven floors/surfaces. Injuries range from minor bruises and abrasions to sprains, dislocations, fractures, and head trauma.

Risk areas: staircases, queue lines, entrance ramps, wet floors.

Impact: may require wound care, immobilisation, or referral to emergency services in cases of suspected fractures or head injuries.

09

Critical Medical Emergencies

Time-sensitive emergencies require immediate assessment and escalation.

EmergencyWatch for
Heart attackSudden chest pain, shortness of breath, or collapse.
StrokeFacial deviation, sudden weakness, or slurred speech.
SeizuresUncontrolled movements, loss of consciousness, or post-seizure confusion.

Impact

These emergencies can occur with little to no warning. Rapid recognition and response are critical for survival and long-term outcomes.

10

Response Protocol

1
Observe

Scan the environment. Identify potential dangers and the nature of the situation.

2
Approach Safely

Ensure the scene is safe for you, others, and the individual.

3
Assess (ABCDE)

Airway · Breathing · Circulation · Disability · Exposure.

4
Provide First Aid

Deliver appropriate care. Prioritise life-threatening conditions.

5
Document

Record what happened, observations, actions taken, and time.

6
Escalate

Notify the right personnel or emergency services. Be clear and concise.

Final Reminder

  • Be proactive. Stay visible. Work as a team.
  • Watch the queues — protect vulnerable guests.
  • Escalate early when in doubt.
  • Observe → Assess → Act → Escalate.
  • Your role combines clinical awareness with spiritual attentiveness.
  • Stay prayerful — you are a watchman.
11

Documenting a Medical Incident

Every guest interaction is someone's whole day. A clear, written record protects them, protects you, and keeps the whole medics chain working as one team.

  • Continuity of care — anyone who picks up the case afterward (Group Lead, Coordinator, stadium medical team) knows exactly what happened and what's already been done.
  • Accountability — a written, timestamped record shows the right steps were followed and the right people were informed.
  • Safeguarding — protects the guest, especially where next-of-kin, hospital transfer, or a vulnerable person is involved.
  • Learning for next time — patterns across incidents help the HOD improve training, staffing, and stadium liaison for future conferences.

Golden rule

One form per incident — even minor ones. If it needed a medical assessment, it needs a form. A quick check that turns out fine still gets logged; it protects you and the guest either way.

13

Incident & Person Details

The who, where, and when — get this down first, exactly as it happened.

1. Incident Details +
  • Date and time — as close to the actual moment as possible.
  • Stand / Block, and row or seat if known — precise location speeds up any stadium escalation.
  • Reported by — your name, as the attending Medic.
  • Group Lead and Coordinator notified — name who you told, not just "yes".
2. Person Details +
  • Name and approx. age, if you can get them without pressuring a distressed guest.
  • Guest / Volunteer / Staff — changes who else needs informing.
  • Known medical conditions — ask gently; this can change your assessment.
  • Next of kin contacted? — mark clearly if this is still outstanding.
14

Presenting Signs

Two checklists, kept separate on purpose.

  • Unresponsive / no chest movement
  • Collapse / loss of consciousness
  • Seizure-like symptoms
  • Difficulty breathing or gasping
  • Chest pain or clutching chest
  • Severe confusion or unresponsiveness
  • Bluish lips or very pale skin

Spiritual manifestation (for context, not diagnosis)

Shaking · Collapse · Agitation · Unresponsiveness
Note context only — this never replaces the medical assessment. Medical checks always come first.

16

Outcome, Sign-off & Before You Go Out

Close the loop — this is what makes the record usable afterward.

  • Resolved on site
  • Returned to seat
  • Transferred to stadium medical
  • Taken to hospital

Sign-off

No names logged, no closed incident. Medic, Group Lead, and Coordinator names are what make the record official.

Before You Go Out on Shift

  • Medical assessment always comes first.
  • One form, every incident, filled in on site.
  • Name who you escalated to — every time.
  • Submit the form on your phone the moment it's handled — don't batch incidents.
  • Save your form link or QR code before your shift starts.
  • Ask your Coordinator if you're ever unsure.
