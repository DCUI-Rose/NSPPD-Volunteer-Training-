01

Role Overview

As a Group Lead, you carry direct, hands-on responsibility for your team and its kit. You take direction from the Medical Coordinators, but on the ground, your team looks to you first — for positioning, for safety, and for knowing exactly what to do when something happens.

  • Two Group Lead roles — Crowd Medic Team Lead (holds the first aid bag) and Roaming Medic Group Lead (accountable for the team kit).
  • You take direction from Coordinators — the Medical Group Lead takes direction from the Medical Coordinators; that's your reporting line.
  • Kit & people are your responsibility — whichever team you lead, the safety of your medics and the accountability of your kit sits with you.

03

Crowd Medic Team Lead

Your team & your post.

  • Team structure — four medics per section, plus you as Team Lead. You are the one who physically holds the first aid bag.
  • First touchpoint — your team is the first point of contact for the ushering team and guests in your section if an emergency arises.
  • Liaison — work directly with the ushering team assigned to your section; they will come to you, and you to them.
  • Flagging — if an incident needs more support than your section can give, flag the nearest medics.
04

What to expect.

Eateries in the concourses

That's where your team goes and returns from.

4

When it isn't your section's turn, you may be covering a neighbouring section.

05

Roaming Medic Group Lead

Your team, your kit.

  • Team size — you lead 4–5 medics as a Roaming Medic Group Lead, with your own operational medical kit.
  • Positioning — support the crowd management team and guest experience at your assigned entrance.
  • Repositioning — once crowd influx has settled, reposition to the concourses or guest experience posts as directed.
  • Breaks — your team breaks once every hour, for 20 minutes, before returning to patrol.
06

Roaming Group Lead Focus — Your Kit Is Your Responsibility

1
Pick up from procurement

Collect your team's kit from the procurement team at the start — sign the sign-out form when you take it.

2
On break, coordinator holds it

Hand the kit to the Roaming Medic Coordinator while your team takes its break.

3
You take it back

When your team returns, the kit comes back to you. Ensure your team is back within the 20 minutes.

4
Return & sign off

At the end of the conference, YOU return the kit to procurement and ensure the sign-out form is updated to confirm the return.

The sign-out form is your paper trail

Update it both when you collect the kit and when you return it.

08

Group Lead Code of Conduct

1
Lead From the Front

Your team follows your example in professionalism, punctuality, and staying calm under pressure.

2
Safeguard First

Testifiers and vulnerable guests are never left unaccompanied. Report any safeguarding concern immediately.

3
Kit Custody

Sign for your kit, keep track of it at all times, and sign it back in. Report anything missing or damaged the same day.

4
Timely Returns

Ensure your team returns from breaks on schedule, so coverage on the ground is never compromised.

5
Documentation

Complete incident forms accurately and promptly — don't leave forms for someone else to finish.

6
Escalate When Needed

If something is beyond your team's scope, escalate to your Coordinator without delay.

09

Code of Conduct — Serving With Character

1
Serve With Joy and Humility

Your role is a stewardship, not a status. Be first to arrive, last to leave, and willing to do what you ask of your medics.

2
Integrity and Honesty

Report accurately to your Coordinator, even when the news is difficult. Own your mistakes without deflecting blame.

3
Diligence and Excellence

Prepare thoroughly before your shift. Follow up — don't assume a task is done until it is confirmed done.

4
Support Without Micromanaging

Check in with your medics, but trust them to do their job. Correct privately, encourage publicly.

5
Gracious Speech

Speak to your medics and your Coordinator with the same graciousness you'd want shown to you. Avoid gossip.

6
Unity and Servant Leadership

Keep the mission — service, healing, and love — at the centre of every decision. Lead by doing, not demanding.

12

Documenting a Medical Incident

Every guest interaction is someone's whole day. A clear, written record protects them, protects you, and keeps the whole medics chain working as one team.

  • Continuity of care — anyone who picks up the case afterward (Group Lead, Coordinator, stadium medical team) knows exactly what happened and what's already been done.
  • Accountability — a written, timestamped record shows the right steps were followed and the right people were informed.
  • Safeguarding — protects the guest, especially where next-of-kin, hospital transfer, or a vulnerable person is involved.
  • Learning for next time — patterns across incidents help the HOD improve training, staffing, and stadium liaison for future conferences.

Golden rule

One form per incident — even minor ones. If it needed a medical assessment, it needs a form. A quick check that turns out fine still gets logged; it protects you and the guest either way.

When, Who & The Form At a Glance

The attending Medic completes the form — the person who assessed the guest, not a passer-by. Fill it in at the time of the incident, while details are fresh, and submit it from your phone immediately, on the spot — don't batch incidents for later.

2

4

6

Medic, Group Lead, Coordinator.

14

Incident & Person Details

The who, where, and when — get this down first, exactly as it happened.

1. Incident Details +
  • Date and time — as close to the actual moment as possible.
  • Stand / Block, and row or seat if known — precise location speeds up any stadium escalation.
  • Reported by — your name, as the attending Medic.
  • Group Lead and Coordinator notified — name who you told, not just "yes".
2. Person Details +
  • Name and approx. age, if you can get them without pressuring a distressed guest.
  • Guest / Volunteer / Staff — changes who else needs informing.
  • Known medical conditions — ask gently; this can change your assessment.
  • Next of kin contacted? — mark clearly if this is still outstanding.
15

Presenting Signs

Two checklists, kept separate on purpose.

  • Unresponsive / no chest movement
  • Collapse / loss of consciousness
  • Seizure-like symptoms
  • Difficulty breathing or gasping
  • Chest pain or clutching chest
  • Severe confusion or unresponsiveness
  • Bluish lips or very pale skin

Spiritual manifestation (for context, not diagnosis)

Shaking · Collapse · Agitation · Unresponsiveness
Note context only — this never replaces the medical assessment. Medical checks always come first.

16

Assessment Order & Escalation

Write your assessment in this order — it's how the notes read fastest for whoever escalates next.

1
Observe

Spot abnormal behaviour before you approach.

2
Approach

Approach safely; make sure the surrounding area is clear.

3
Check Responsiveness

Tap shoulder, call name. No response → begin ABCDE and escalate to your Group Lead.

4
ABCDE

Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure — write down what you find at each step.

→Ushers

Flag to nearest Medic.

↓
→Medics

Assess, document, and decide.

↓
→Coordinators

Notified, flag stadium team.

↓
→Stadium Medical (Cipher)

Takes over if needed.

17

Outcome, Sign-off & Before You Go Out

Close the loop — this is what makes the record usable afterward.

  • Resolved on site
  • Returned to seat
  • Transferred to stadium medical
  • Taken to hospital

Sign-off

No names logged, no closed incident. Medic, Group Lead, and Coordinator names are what make the record official.

Before You Go Out on Shift

  • Medical assessment always comes first.
  • One form, every incident, filled in on site.
  • Name who you escalated to — every time.
  • Submit the form on your phone the moment it's handled — don't batch incidents.
  • Save your form link or QR code before your shift starts.
  • Ask your Coordinator if you're ever unsure.
